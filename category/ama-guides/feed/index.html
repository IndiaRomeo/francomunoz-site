Back injuries are some of the most common — and most misunderstood — injuries in California workers’ compensation cases. Many injured workers assume that once they report a back injury, the insurance company will fairly evaluate their pain, limitations, and long-term impact. Unfortunately, that is rarely how it works.

If you’ve hurt your back at work, the value of your case is not automatic. It depends heavily on how the case is documented, treated, and legally positioned from the very beginning. Below are key strategies that can significantly increase the value of your back injury case.

1. Report the Injury Immediately and Clearly

One of the fastest ways to weaken a back injury claim is delaying the report. Insurance companies often argue that back pain is “degenerative” or unrelated to work — especially when there is a gap between the injury and the report.

Report your injury as soon as possible and be specific. Explain what you were doing, how your back was injured, and what symptoms you felt immediately. Consistency from day one protects your credibility and strengthens causation.

2. Get Proper Medical Treatment — Not Just Any Treatment

Not all medical care is created equal. Insurance companies rely heavily on medical records to determine the value of your case. If your treatment is minimal, inconsistent, or poorly documented, your case value will suffer.

Back injury cases are stronger when treatment includes:

  • Diagnostic imaging (MRI, CT scans)

  • Physical therapy

  • Pain management or injections

  • Specialist evaluations (orthopedic or spine doctors)

More importantly, your doctor must clearly document functional limitations, work restrictions, and how the injury affects your daily activities.

3. Follow All Work Restrictions Carefully

Insurance companies watch closely to see whether injured workers follow medical restrictions. If your doctor says no lifting, limited standing, or modified duties — follow those instructions exactly.

Violating restrictions can:

  • Reduce the credibility of your injury

  • Be used to argue you are not truly disabled

  • Lower your permanent disability rating

Compliance helps show the injury has real consequences and limitations.

4. Understand the Importance of Permanent Disability Ratings

The long-term value of most back injury cases depends on permanent disability (PD). PD ratings are based on how much your injury permanently affects your ability to work and live normally.

Cases become more valuable when:

  • Multiple levels of the spine are involved

  • There are neurological symptoms (radiating pain, numbness, weakness)

  • The injury limits standing, walking, lifting, or sitting for long periods

A well-supported permanent disability rating can dramatically increase settlement value.

5. Don’t Overlook Future Medical Care

Future medical care is often where real value lies in a back injury case. Chronic back injuries frequently require:

  • Ongoing pain management

  • Future injections or procedures

  • Long-term physical therapy

  • Possible surgery

Insurance companies will try to minimize future care. Strong medical opinions supporting future treatment can substantially raise the value of your case — especially in lump-sum settlements.

6. Avoid Insurance Company Traps

Adjusters are trained to look for ways to devalue claims. Recorded statements, surveillance, and selective medical opinions are common tactics.

Before giving statements or agreeing to evaluations, it’s critical to understand how these actions can impact your case value.

7. Hire the Right Workers’ Compensation Lawyer

Perhaps the single most important factor in increasing the value of a back injury case is experienced legal representation. A knowledgeable workers’ compensation attorney knows how to:

  • Develop strong medical evidence

  • Challenge unfair medical opinions

  • Maximize permanent disability

  • Protect future medical benefits

  • Negotiate higher settlements

Back injury cases are not “one size fits all.” Strategy matters.

Call to Action: Protect the Value of Your Case

If you suffered a back injury at work, do not assume the insurance company will treat you fairly. The steps you take right now can determine whether your case is worth thousands — or hundreds of thousands — of dollars.

In a significant decision by the Workers’ Compensation Appeals Board (WCAB) involving Sammy Vigil versus the County of Kern, the intricacies of impairment evaluation and apportionment were thoroughly examined. This case sheds light on the complexities of determining permanent disability in workers’ compensation claims and highlights why it’s crucial for injured workers to have expert legal representation.

Case Summary

Sammy Vigil, a maintenance painter for the County of Kern, sustained injuries to his hips and back, leading to bilateral hip replacement surgeries. Following these surgeries, a Qualified Medical Evaluator (QME), Dr. Peter Newton, assessed Vigil’s impairments and assigned whole-person impairment (WPI) ratings of 15% for each hip and 7% for the lumbar spine. Dr. Newton recommended adding the impairments for each hip, rather than combining them using the Combined Values Chart (CVC), due to the synergistic effect of the bilateral impairments.

 

Key Issues and Rulings

  1. Impairment Rating and Synergy: The WCAB’s en banc decision emphasized that the CVC, which combines impairment ratings to account for overlapping effects on activities of daily living (ADLs), can be rebutted. In Vigil’s case, the QME argued that having impairments in both hips resulted in greater limitations than if the impairments were assessed separately. The WCAB agreed that impairments could be added if it was shown that the impairments’ effects on ADLs did not overlap or created a synergistic effect.
  2. Apportionment and Surgical Outcomes: The case also delved into the apportionment of disability, especially following surgical interventions. The WCAB found that Dr. Newton’s apportionment opinion was not substantial evidence because it did not adequately explain what non-industrial factors contributed to the need for hip replacements. This decision clarified that successful industrial surgeries do not automatically exempt the resulting disability from apportionment, but detailed medical reasoning is required to establish non-industrial causation.

Practical Implications

This case underscores the importance of detailed medical evaluations and the necessity for workers to understand their rights under workers’ compensation law. The nuances of impairment evaluation, particularly when surgeries are involved, can significantly impact the disability benefits an injured worker receives.

For injured workers, this decision highlights several critical points:

  • Detailed Medical Evidence: Ensuring that medical reports are thorough and well-reasoned is essential. In Vigil’s case, the lack of detailed explanation from the QME on the impacts of ADLs and the non-industrial causation of impairments played a pivotal role in the WCAB’s decision.
  • Understanding Synergy: Workers and their representatives should be aware that the combined impact of multiple impairments can sometimes justify higher disability ratings. Demonstrating how impairments amplify limitations on daily activities can be crucial.
  • Legal Representation: Navigating the complexities of workers’ compensation claims, especially with issues like impairment rating and apportionment, often requires expert legal assistance. Knowledgeable attorneys can help ensure that workers receive the full benefits they are entitled to by thoroughly developing the medical record and challenging inadequate evidence.

]]> Traumatic brain injuries (TBI) are very serious injuries and understanding the etiologies of brain damage from a workers’ compensation perspective requires a fundamental understanding of the interplay between neurology and psychiatry. TBI indutrial injuries will usually require a medical-legal (QME) evaluation by a neurologist and a psychiatrist or psychologist given the types of disabilities that may arise from a TBI or a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI).

Chapter 13 of the American Medical Association (AMA) Medical Guides, 5th Edition addresses potential cranial nerve damage and peripheral nervous damage corresponding to a TBI. Generally, the cranial nerve one is the most affected nerve, however, it is generally not properly tested by primary treating physicians (PTPs) and we always work with PTPs to ensure that cranial nerve 1 is tested appropriately. To a lesser extent, cranial nerve 7 (involving the facial nerve for motor strength) and cranial 3 (involving the pupil area) are also impacted by a TBI. Further, Chapter 12 of the AMA Guides addresses problems corresponding to ambien vision (side vision); and Chapter 13 of the AMA Guides addresses the vestibular aspects of an injury. Other chapters of the AMA Guides also touch on injuries caused by TBIs that we will address in a future blog post.

It should be noted that often times valium is used over time to treat TBIs, however, extensive usage can result in a decrease in memory — in these instances, it may be useful for a QME to review Chapter 11 of the AMA Guides as it relates to one’s functional efficiency and audibility.

In terms of determining the whole person impairment (WPI) and level of permanent disability, our position is that the addition of disabilities under the Kite case is the appropriate approach to determine the WPI because of the vision, hearing, and disequilibirum concurrency that is found in most serious TBIs.

Lastly, to briefly address medical treatment, it should be noted that the Administrative Director has incorporated the Medical Treatment Utilization Schedule (MTUS). When an employee is injured at work, he or she is entitled to receive reasonable and necessary medical treatment to cure or relieve the injury. The Medical Treatment Utilization Schedule (MTUS) is a set of regulations found in title 8, California Code of Regulations section 9792.20 through 9792.27.23 that contain medical treatment guidelines and rules for determining what is reasonable and necessary medical care. The MTUS is based on the principles of evidence-based medicine. That means treatment decisions are guided by recommendations supported by the best-available evidence. Of note, there are two types of drugs — (a) there are exempt drugs that don’t require authorization but need to be prescribed pursuant to the MTUS; and (b) non-exempt drugs that require authorization by Utilization Review like most other drugs.
